A management buy-out (MBO) is the purchase of a company by its own managers. They set up a holding company, put in part of their savings and top up with bank debt, often an investment fund and sometimes a vendor loan. Knowing the business inside out cuts some risks. It does not cut the risk of overpaying.

## What exactly is an MBO?

A takeover from the inside. The managers who already run the business become its shareholders, through a holding that borrows to buy the shares.

The seller gets continuity: the company passes to people who know the customers, the teams and the machines. The bank and the fund are looking at a better-known risk than a buyer nobody has met. It is an LBO where the buyers are employees.

## How is an MBO financed?

From four sources, inside a holding. The example below is built for illustration and does not come from a real deal: purchase of 100% of an SME for €4M.

| Source | Amount | Share of price | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| Managers' contribution to the holding | €300,000 | 7.5% | Personal savings, sometimes partly funded by a personal loan |
| Fund's contribution (equity or quasi-equity) | €1,200,000 | 30% | Minority in the holding |
| Bank loan to the holding | €2,000,000 | 50% | Repaid from the target's dividends |
| Vendor loan | €500,000 | 12.5% | Paid over several years |
| **Total** | **€4,000,000** | **100%** | |

The debt must stay in line with what the company can pay out in dividends every year, after its own investment needs. What the bank is willing to lend is not always what the target can carry without damage.

## How much of the company do the managers keep?

It all depends on how much the fund puts in as equity and how much as quasi-equity.

As a percentage: if the fund invests its €1.2M in shares at the same price as the managers, the holding has €1.5M of share capital and the managers own €300,000 of it, or 20%. The fund owns 80%. It is no longer a minority at all.

To keep the managers in charge, the fund puts most of its ticket into a subordinated loan or bond. If, in the same example, it takes only €250,000 in shares and €950,000 in quasi-equity, the managers own €300,000 out of €550,000, or 54.5%. Same money in, reversed control. The price of that choice shows up in what the quasi-equity pays, which you can compare in our piece on the [subordinated loan](/en/blog/fonds-propres-ou-dette/subordinated-loan).

Reading shareholders' agreements taught me to skip the amount line. The one that matters is the split between shares and loan.

## How much must the target pay out each year?

A €2M loan over seven years, the term of the bank loan in a takeover case published by Wallonie Entreprendre (WE, the Walloon Region's public financing body), means about €286,000 of principal repayment a year. Interest comes on top. So does the vendor loan, depending on its schedule.

That money leaves the target as dividends. If the company generates €400,000 a year before investment, almost nothing is left to replace a machine.

## What are the steps of an MBO?

1. Agreement in principle: the seller agrees to consider an MBO.
2. Preparation: the buyers' business plan, a first valuation.
3. Financing round: bank, fund, possible vendor loan.
4. [Letter of intent](/en/blog/lever-des-fonds/letter-of-intent-and-term-sheet): price, structure, timetable, exclusivity.
5. Due diligence and contracts: share purchase agreement, shareholders' agreement, loans.
6. Closing and handover: share transfer, transition.

## What risks do the buying managers take?

Not where you would expect. The business risk is well known; the personal risk less so.

| Situation | What happens | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| You borrow privately for your contribution | Your personal loan runs even if the holding pays nothing | Keep the borrowed part to what your salary alone can repay |
| The bank asks for a personal guarantee | The holding's risk moves onto your private assets | Negotiate a cap, have a lawyer review it |
| One manager leaves after two years | Leaver clause: forced sale of his or her shares, sometimes below value | Read the [exit clauses](/en/blog/lever-des-fonds/exit-clauses-tag-drag-leaver) before signing |
| The target cannot pay a dividend | The holding stops repaying, the bank turns to its collateral | Keep a cash buffer in year one |
| You negotiate the price with your own boss | Reporting line, unequal information | Negotiate with an adviser and an investor beside you |

Moving from manager to owner-manager is the last risk, and the most underrated. A strong production director can discover after closing that the seller alone held the relationship with the bank and the three largest customers.

## Does company law set limits?

Yes, two. In an SRL (the Belgian private limited company), a distribution is only possible if net assets remain positive and the board confirms the company can pay its debts for at least twelve months ([Art. 5:142 and 5:143 of the Code of Companies and Associations](https://www.ejustice.just.fgov.be/eli/loi/2019/03/23/2019A40586/justel)).

The target may also fund or guarantee the purchase of its own shares only under Art. 5:152 (SRL) or 7:227 (SA): general meeting approval, a board report, distributable reserves.

<Warning>A pledge over the target's assets in favour of the holding's bank, without the Art. 5:152 procedure, is weak security. The flaw comes to light the day the bank tries to enforce it.</Warning>

## What role does the fund play in an MBO?

Money first, and three things around it:

- it helps set a valuation that the seller, the bank and the buyers can defend;
- it adds weight in the negotiation with the seller;
- it organises governance between several manager-shareholders, so they do not discover their disagreements at the first board meeting.

[ORSA](https://orsafund.be/reprendre-une-pme/), for example, publishes a "Reprendre une PME" (buy an SME) track for Walloon industrial SMEs with 20 to 150 staff, with €500,000 to €2.5M in equity (read on 15.10.2026). The other players, with their tickets, are in the [ranking of funds for SMEs](/en/classement/fonds-investissement-pme). In Flanders, [PMV lends for MBOs](https://www.vlaio.be/nl/subsidies-financiering/subsidiedatabank/pmv-bedrijfsleningen) from €700,000, covering at most 50% of the need.
