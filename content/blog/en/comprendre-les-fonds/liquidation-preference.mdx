A liquidation preference gives the fund the right to be paid before you when the company is sold: its money first, sometimes a multiple of its money, sometimes its share of the remainder on top. It changes nothing in your percentage on the share register. It changes the cheque. And it weighs most on average sales, which are the most common outcome for an SME.

## What is a liquidation preference?

It is an order of payment. On the day the company is sold, or wound up, the price is not split pro rata to the shares: it goes down a staircase. The fund takes the first step, up to what the clause says, and the rest flows down to the ordinary shareholders, meaning you.

The word "liquidation" is misleading. A term sheet almost never talks about bankruptcy: it defines a "liquidation event" covering the sale of a majority of the shares, a merger, the sale of the business, sometimes a stock market listing. In other words, the exit you are hoping for.

I say this up front because it is the misunderstanding I have seen most often while reading shareholders' agreements from the owner's chair: the clause is not insurance against the worst case. It sets how a successful sale is split when the sale is not quite successful enough.

## The clause in eight terms

Term sheets are often drafted in English, even between Namur and Charleroi. Here are the words you will read, and the effect of each on your share of the price.

| Term | One-sentence definition | Effect for the owner |
|---|---|---|
| Liquidation preference | The fund's right to be paid first on a liquidation event | Takes a first slice of the price before any split |
| Multiple (1x, 2x) | Priority amount expressed as a multiple of the fund's investment | 2x on €1.2M = €2.4M paid before you |
| Non-participating | The fund chooses: its preference or its pro rata share, never both | The clause switches off above a certain price |
| Participating | The fund takes its preference, then its share of the remainder | The clause costs you at every sale price |
| Cap | Maximum total a participating fund can receive, often 2x or 3x its money | Limits the double helping on large sales |
| Conversion | The fund's option to give up its preference and be treated as an ordinary shareholder | Explains why the clause stops applying |
| Seniority | Ranking between the preferences of several rounds | The last investor in is often paid first |
| Pari passu | Preferences from different rounds are paid together, pro rata | Shares the shortfall between investors |

A benchmark to fix ideas: of the more than 400 European venture and growth rounds handled by the law firm Orrick in 2025, 77% carried a 1x non-participating preference, and fewer than 1% a participating one ([Orrick, Deal Flow 6.0, published 31 March 2026](https://www.orrick.com/en/Insights/2026/03/Deal-Flow-6-5-Things-We-Learned-About-European-Tech-Deal-Terms-in-2025)).

## A fund at 30%, four sale prices: the calculation

An example built for illustration, not a real case. An industrial SME in Hainaut raises €1.2M from a fund on a €2.8M pre-money. Post-money: €4M. The fund holds 30%, the owner 70%. A few years later, a trade buyer acquires 100% of the shares. I vary two things: the sale price and the clause.

Amount received by the owner, in millions of euros:

| Fund's clause | Sale at €2.4M | Sale at €4M | Sale at €6M | Sale at €8M |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| None (ordinary shares) | 1.68 | 2.80 | 4.20 | 5.60 |
| 1x non-participating | 1.20 | 2.80 | 4.20 | 5.60 |
| 2x non-participating | 0 | 1.60 | 3.60 | 5.60 |
| 1x participating | 0.84 | 1.96 | 3.36 | 4.76 |
| 1x participating, 2x cap | 0.84 | 1.96 | 3.60 | 5.60 |

Read the €2.4M column. The company sold for 60% of the post-money, which happens. With no clause, you receive €1.68M. With a 2x non-participating preference, you receive nothing: the fund takes the whole €2.4M, twice its investment, and you have worked for it.

Then read the €8M column. Four lines out of five come back to €5.6M, because 30% of €8M is €2.4M and the fund now has every reason to convert. Only the uncapped participating preference still costs the owner €840,000.

Hence the rule I apply to every term sheet: a non-participating preference stops applying above its multiple times the post-money. At 1x, the post-money. At 2x, double. Below that threshold, every euro of preference is a euro taken from your share.

## Participating or non-participating: which costs more?

Participating, by far. It pays the fund twice: its money on the first step, then 30% of what is left. On the €4M sale in the example, that is €1.2M plus 30% of €2.8M, so €2.04M for a fund holding 30% of the shares. It takes 51% of the price.

A cap fixes part of the problem. With a 2x cap, the participating fund never goes above €2.4M unless it converts. Between a €5.2M and an €8M sale price, it is stuck at the cap, and every extra euro goes to the ordinary shareholders. Above €8M, it converts and becomes a 30% shareholder again.

The cap changes nothing below €5.2M, which is where most SME sales happen. That is why I do not see it as a sufficient concession. It makes a participating preference acceptable on a great exit and leaves its cost untouched on an ordinary one.

## 77% of rounds at 1x non-participating: is the clause harmless?

The objection is serious, and it often comes from the fund itself: "it's market standard, 1x non-participating, nobody negotiates that". Orrick's figures prove it right on the first point. Three European rounds out of four carry exactly this clause.

Two caveats, though.

First, the sample. These are tech venture rounds in Europe's large technology markets, aiming at exits several times the post-money. A Walloon SME with 40 staff, sold to a competitor or handed over to a buyer, does not live in that distribution. Its most likely exit sits around the post-money, sometimes below, and that is exactly the zone where a 1x bites.

Second, the standard only covers the multiple. Ranking between rounds, the definition of a liquidation event, accrued dividends or interest on the preference: none of this is standardised, and that is where clauses get heavier without changing their label. A "1x non-participating" preference with an 8% cumulative annual dividend becomes, after five years, a preference of 1.4x to 1.47x depending on whether the dividend compounds.

My view: a 1x non-participating preference is acceptable when the valuation is fair. It becomes expensive when you accept it in exchange for an inflated pre-money, because it then raises the threshold below which you lose money. The [anti-dilution clause](/en/blog/lever-des-fonds/anti-dilution-and-ratchet) follows the same logic: it strikes when the company is doing less well.

## Two rounds, two preferences: who is paid first?

Back to the SME. Two years after the first round, it raises €2M from a second fund, also on a 1x non-participating preference. Then it is sold for €2.8M. The preferences add up to €3.2M, more than the price.

| Order of preferences | Second fund (€2M) | First fund (€1.2M) | Owner |
|---|---|---|---|
| Second round paid first (seniority) | €2.00M | €0.80M | €0 |
| Pari passu, pro rata to preferences | €1.75M | €1.05M | €0 |

The owner gets nothing in either case. Seniority moves money between investors; it gives you nothing back. The figure to remember is the other one: the sum of the preferences, €3.2M, is the sale price below which you do not receive a single euro. I have it recalculated after every round, just like the cap table.

## In Belgium, the clause is written in two places

The Belgian Code of Companies and Associations ([CSA, consolidated text on ejustice](https://www.ejustice.just.fgov.be/eli/loi/2019/03/23/2019A40586/justel)) lets the articles of association of an SRL (private limited company) or an SA (public limited company) create share classes with different rights: preferred dividend, multiple voting rights, non-voting shares, rights to the liquidation surplus, as the [OECCBB](https://blog.oeccbb.be/fr/article/types-dactions-de-la-srl-et-droits-y-relatifs/29401) (the Belgian order of chartered accountants and tax advisers) points out. A fund's preference therefore first takes the form of a share class, written into the articles, which requires a notarial deed.

But the articles only govern liquidation in the strict sense, that of a dissolved company. On a share sale, the buyer pays each seller for their shares: nothing in the articles reallocates that price. The staircase split then comes from the shareholders' agreement, in which each party undertakes to divide the sale proceeds according to the agreed waterfall, often combined with the drag-along clause described in the article on [exit clauses](/en/blog/lever-des-fonds/exit-clauses-tag-drag-leaver).

A preference written on one side only leaves a gap. Tax treatment also differs between a liquidation surplus and the price of a share sale: have your accountant run the numbers before you sign, not when you sell.

## Belgian funds publish their ticket sizes, not their preferences

I took the conditions that four players covered on this site publish themselves. None publishes its practice on liquidation preferences: you read it in the term sheet, deal by deal.

| Player | Published ticket | Published instruments | Area | Liquidation preference |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| ORSA | €500,000 to €2.5M | equity, quasi-equity, Smart Bond (subordinated loan, 5 to 10 years) | Wallonia | not published |
| Theodorus | €250,000 to €2.5M | seed equity | spin-offs and deep tech, fund linked to ULB | not published |
| Noshaq | not published | equity, loans, leasing depending on the entity | province of Liège | not published |
| finance&invest.brussels | up to €5M | loans, guarantees, equity stakes | Brussels-Capital Region | not published |

Checked on the funds' websites on 15/10/2026. The [SME funds ranking](/en/classement/fonds-investissement-pme) and the [comparison tool](/en/comparer/fonds-investissement-pme) cover the other players, with the same columns.

One detail matters in this table. ORSA's Smart Bond is a subordinated loan, like the offer of several provincial invests: the lender is repaid before all shareholders because it is a creditor, and it takes no share of the sale price beyond what it is still owed. No waterfall to negotiate, interest to pay. The [subordinated loan](/en/blog/fonds-propres-ou-dette/subordinated-loan) has its downsides, but on this precise point it is easier to read than a preference share.

## Four points to have written down before signing

- A 1x multiple, non-participating. If the fund insists on participating, a cap of 2x at most, spelt out in full.
- A closed definition of the liquidation event: sale of more than 50% of the shares, merger, sale of the business. Not "any similar transaction".
- No cumulative dividend or interest that inflates the preference over time, or else a ceiling.
- A distribution table annexed to the agreement, on three sale prices: half the post-money, the post-money, double.

<Verdict>The liquidation preference is negotiated at the same time as the valuation, never afterwards. A flattering pre-money with a heavy preference can leave you with less, on an ordinary sale, than a modest pre-money with no clause.
