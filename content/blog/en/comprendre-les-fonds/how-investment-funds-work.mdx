An investment fund gives an SME equity or quasi-equity in exchange for a share of the capital or a return tied to results. It is not repaid like a bank: it makes its money when it sells. In Wallonia and Brussels, published tickets range from €100,000 to €5M.

## What is an investment fund for an SME?

Seen from your desk, an investment fund is a professional shareholder or lender. It manages money from its own backers (institutions, entrepreneurs, public bodies) and puts it into unlisted companies. It takes on a risk your bank turns down. And it charges for that risk in something other than interest.

That risk is priced in three ways, depending on the instrument:

- equity: the fund subscribes to new shares and earns its return when it sells them;
- quasi-equity: a subordinated or convertible loan, repaid after other debts, with a higher rate or a variable component;
- a mix of the two, which is very common in SME buyouts.

The word covers structures that have almost nothing in common. A provincial "invest" (a regional public-private investment company) such as Noshaq in Liège, a private evergreen fund such as ORSA, a regional body such as Wallonie Entreprendre (the Walloon Region's public financing arm), a seed fund linked to the ULB university such as Theodorus: they all "invest", and none of them works the same way. Four questions are enough to tell them apart, and I ask them before I even open a brochure.

| Question to ask | Why it matters to you |
|---|---|
| Does the fund have an end date? | A closed-end fund must sell before its term; an evergreen fund picks its moment |
| Who are its shareholders? | A public invest has a regional mission; a private fund follows its own strategy |
| At what stage does it invest? | Seed, growth, succession: risks and ticket sizes differ |
| What ticket does it publish? | Outside that range, your file will not be reviewed |

An edge case makes this concrete. A 12-person SME looking for €300,000 will not get through ORSA's door: its published ticket starts at €500,000 and it targets SMEs with 20 to 150 employees. If the company is based in the province of Namur, Namur Invest goes down to €100,000. Same need, two opposite answers, and the only thing separating them is printed in black and white on each fund's website.

## Nine funds surveyed on 15.10.2026: four publish no ticket

On 15 October 2026 I recorded, from their own websites, what nine funds active in Wallonia and Brussels say about their ticket, their area and their instruments. The most useful finding is a gap.

| Fund | Area | Published ticket | Published instruments |
|---|---|---|---|
| ORSA (formerly Investsud) | Wallonia | €500,000 to €2,500,000 | Equity, quasi-equity, Smart Bond (subordinated loan over 5 to 10 years) |
| Namur Invest | Province of Namur | €100,000 to €2.5M in general | Equity, convertible, subordinated, unsecured, secured and bullet loans, property leasing |
| Invest.BW (formerly Nivelinvest) | Walloon Brabant | Up to €2.5M in total, over one or more rounds | Equity, subordinated or mezzanine loan, convertible loan |
| Sambrinvest | Charleroi Métropole | not published | Risk capital, subordinated, convertible, participating and ordinary loans |
| Noshaq (formerly Meusinvest) | Province of Liège | not published | Equity, loans, leasing depending on the group entity |
| IMBC | Mons, Borinage and Centre | not published | not specified on the pages reviewed |
| Wallonie Entreprendre | Wallonia | not published | Equity, loans, guarantees; funding of the provincial invests |
| finance&invest.brussels | Brussels-Capital Region | Up to €5M for SMEs, as co-investment | Loans, guarantees, equity, "prêt proxi" via its subsidiary brusoc |
| Theodorus | Belgium, opening to Canada (Theodorus IV) | €250,000 on first investment, up to €2.5M | Equity, seed and early stage |

Four of the nine publish no ticket. That is not a criticism: an invest that finances everything from start-ups to established SMEs has no single range to display. But "not published" does not mean "no limit". It means you will learn the floor and the ceiling at the first meeting, after preparing a full file.

The second finding was less expected. Among the five published ceilings, four stop at the same figure: €2.5M at ORSA, Namur Invest, Invest.BW and Theodorus. Only finance&invest.brussels states up to €5M for an SME, and only as a co-investment alongside a bank or a private fund. A €4M need in Wallonia is therefore almost always put together by several funders.

Published sizes give a sense of what each player can carry. ORSA says it has been active since 1985, has backed more than 300 companies and manages more than €120M. Sambrinvest reports more than 35 years of experience, close to 300 SMEs in its portfolio and more than €200M under management. Noshaq publishes an average of €120M in investments approved per year over the last five years, with 60 staff. Theodorus manages more than €70M. Wallonie Entreprendre publishes a portfolio valued at €4.1bn, including €1bn invested in the invests that in turn finance local SMEs.

## How does a fund make money?

In equity, it makes money on exit. In quasi-equity, it earns interest and often a variable component tied to your results. Everything else follows from that difference.

A fund holding shares earns nothing until it sells them, apart from dividends, which are rare in an SME that reinvests. It knows some of its holdings will fail and makes up for them with the ones that do well. Hence its interest in the exit price and timing, which it wants fixed at signing.

A fictional example shows the mechanism plainly. The fund invests €1M and receives 25% of the capital. Six years later, the company is worth €8M: its 25% is worth €2M, so it has doubled its money before fees and tax. If the company is worth €4M, it gets €1M back and has earned nothing in six years. You can see why the exit clause runs to three pages of the shareholders' agreement while the percentage takes one line.

Quasi-equity works differently. ORSA describes its Smart Bond as a subordinated loan over 5 to 10 years, non-dilutive, with a return partly linked to EBITDA. The fund does not become a shareholder. It earns more if you earn more, and you keep 100% of your shares.

One figure published by Namur Invest says a lot about what provincial invests really do: its portfolio is split roughly 20% equity and 80% debt, with three to four interventions per company on average. In other words, a provincial invest earns its living mostly as a lender. The owner who walks in thinking "I'm going to open up my capital" often walks out with a subordinated loan, and sometimes that is the best outcome for them.

<Tip>Ask the fund at the very first meeting how much of its investment would be equity and how much would be a loan. The answer sets your dilution before anyone talks about valuation.</Tip>

## What stake does a fund take?

The stake depends on two numbers: the amount invested and your company's value before the investment (the pre-money valuation). The formula fits on one line: fund's stake = amount invested ÷ (pre-money valuation + amount invested).

As percentages, for the same €1M investment:

| Pre-money valuation | Post-money value | Fund's stake | Stake you keep |
|---|---|---|---|
| €1.5M | €2.5M | 40% | 60% |
| €3M | €4M | 25% | 75% |
| €6M | €7M | 14.3% | 85.7% |

The same sum costs 14% or 40% of your company depending on the valuation. That is why I get wary when an owner tells me "we negotiated the amount". The amount is rarely negotiated. The valuation is.

In SMEs, most deals are minority stakes. Control is mainly handed over in a leveraged buyout or when the owner organises their exit. But a minority stake does not mean no power. A fund at 25% with a veto on any investment above €250,000 weighs more, day to day, than a 35% partner with no special rights. If a letter of intent offers a stake that feels heavy, the article on [a fund asking for 40% of your company](/en/blog/lever-des-fonds/fund-wants-40-percent-of-my-company) covers what gets negotiated around the number.

Quasi-equity changes the calculation entirely: 0% dilution. In return, it sits on the liabilities side, has to be repaid, and its variable component can get expensive if EBITDA climbs. The cost of financing always shows up somewhere: in the percentage for equity, in the income statement for quasi-equity.

## Wallonia, Brussels, Flanders: three different public gateways

The answer to "who will finance my SME" depends first on your address. Each region has built its own body, and access rules do not carry over from one region to the next.

In Wallonia, [Wallonie Entreprendre](https://www.wallonie-entreprendre.be/fr/a-propos/) is the Region's public financing arm. It provides equity, loans and guarantees, and it funds the provincial invests, which are the local entry points: Noshaq in the province of Liège, Sambrinvest in Charleroi, Namur Invest, Invest.BW in Walloon Brabant, IMBC for Mons, the Borinage and the Centre. Their ownership varies. Namur Invest, for instance, describes itself as a private, commercially driven investment company, 60% privately owned and 40% owned by the Walloon Region through Wallonie Entreprendre. Alongside them, private funds such as ORSA cover the whole of Wallonia.

In Brussels, [finance&invest.brussels](https://finance.brussels/) is the public financing body of the Brussels-Capital Region. It provides loans, guarantees and equity, up to €5M for an SME as co-investment, and its subsidiary brusoc offers the "prêt proxi" micro-loan. It positions itself as a complement to banks and private funds, never alone.

In Flanders, the public equivalent is PMV (ParticipatieMaatschappij Vlaanderen). I did not include it in my survey: this site covers Wallonia and Brussels, and I do not quote a ticket I have not checked myself. A Flemish SME starts there. Regional schemes and bodies are covered in detail on the page about [funds and public financing by region](/en/blog/fonds-par-region/funds-and-public-financing-by-region).

## Bank or fund: the difference seen from the credit desk

I spent eight years, from 2009 to 2017, in the business lending department of a bank in Namur. On the side that turns files down. The most common reason was not a bad project but a balance sheet that could not take one more loan: equity too thin, collateral already pledged, debt ratio at the ceiling the committee would accept.

A bank lends and gets repaid on a schedule, against collateral. A fund becomes a shareholder or lends on a subordinated basis. That is where the two jobs meet: the equity a fund puts in strengthens the balance sheet, which then lets the bank lend more. In many of the files I reviewed, the fund was not the alternative to credit, it was the condition for it.

I then worked as financial controller at an industrial SME in Hainaut when a provincial invest came into the capital. I read the shareholders' agreement from the owner's chair, and the line that mattered most was not the percentage. It was the clause setting how, and at what price, the invest would exit.

<Warning>A flattering pre-money valuation can hide clauses that claw it back: preference shares, anti-dilution, guaranteed exit price. Have your accountant or a lawyer review the letter of intent before you sign it, not after.</Warning>

## Five checks before the first meeting

- Does the published ticket cover your need? If the fund does not publish one, ask for the range in writing before sending a full file.
- Is your registered office in the fund's area? Provincial invests are territorial: a Namur company will not go to Noshaq.
- Does the fund have an end date? An evergreen fund such as ORSA imposes no exit horizon; a closed-end fund must sell before its term.
- What mix of equity and quasi-equity does it offer? That mix sets the stake you keep after the round.
- Who does it co-invest with? Invest.BW, for example, says it never finances the whole need on its own.

## Placement funds: the namesake to rule out now

The same word covers two realities. A placement fund (SICAV, UCI, ETF) collects savings from individuals and mostly invests them on the stock market. It is supervised by the [FSMA](https://www.fsma.be/fr) (Financial Services and Markets Authority, the Belgian markets regulator). The investment fund this site covers finances unlisted companies, for several years.

If you landed here looking for somewhere to put your savings, the page on [placement funds versus investment funds](/en/blog/comprendre-les-fonds/placement-fund-vs-investment-fund) points you to neutral information. Here, we talk about the stake you keep after the round.
